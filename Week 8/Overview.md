The Flinders Petrie notebook adds five of Petrie's books from Project Gutenberg on top of GPT-2. When I ran it, Gutenberg was down, so the files the notebook downloaded were empty and the model had only 7 tokens to learn from. Training could not start, and I could not generate anything from the prompt "Local workers are." The model depends on a corpus that someone else hosts, and when that site went down there was nothing to train on. Dr. Graham said in class that "LLMs are a human artifact," and his slides describe an archaeology of generative AI as "layers of dead data, pattern detection throughout." In this notebook Petrie's books would have been the top layer and GPT-2 the layer under it, and the top layer never arrived.

Magnani and Clindaniel found the same problem at a much bigger scale. When ChatGPT and DALL-E 3 described and drew a Neanderthal, the results matched scholarship from the 1960s to the early 1990s, because copyright and paywalls decide which texts are freely available to learn from. The model can only repeat what was collected.

In Teachable Machine I trained a model on 5 red-figure and 5 black-figure images from the Met. It called both red-figure test vases black-figure, one of them with 95% confidence, and only got the black-figure vase right. The categories were mine, but the model decided for itself what separated them, and nothing in its output shows what that was. It was not simply the overall colour, since most of the red-figure training vases are mostly black and several of the black-figure ones are mostly orange. With only 5 images per class, it probably learned something other than whether the figures were painted black or left in the colour of the clay.

The ArchaeoCLIP notebook can search for the idea of something, so a phrase and an image sit in the same space. That could be useful for searching large collections of photographs that were never catalogued in detail. But the closest match is not necessarily an answer, which is Dr. Graham's question about the query layer. When I searched for "warrior with a spear," every vase scored between 0.26 and 0.30, so the model returned a ranking even though the differences between images were very small. The one vase that did show a warrior with a spear ranked ninth, below a woman with two bulls. It also placed the red-figure side of the Andokides amphora closest to a black-figure vase, which is the same mistake Teachable Machine made with a different kind of model.

Labour: these tools rest on other people's work that does not show up in the result. Petrie's books are on Project Gutenberg because volunteers transcribed them. ArchaeoCLIP was trained on several thousand archaeological photographs and their captions, which archaeologists took and described. Petrie's own excavations also depended on Egyptian workers, which is why the prompt "Local workers are" is uncomfortable. Kansa adds that AI scraper bots overload open-access repositories such as Open Context, so the people who keep those repositories running pay part of the cost.

Teachable Machine would not load in Safari, the ArchaeoCLIP notebook expected .jpg files in one folder, and the Petrie notebook failed because Gutenberg was down and its download cell hides errors.
